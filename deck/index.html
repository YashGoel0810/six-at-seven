01 — The problem

Founders are surrounded by people
and alone anyway.

Two distinct pains, usually treated as one. They are not. They need different products — which is exactly why this is two phases.

Pain A — isolation
Nobody to
say it to

You cannot tell your team you are scared, your investors that it is not working, or your friends what an ARR miss feels like. The peer conversation has no venue.

Existing answer: conferences and WhatsApp groups. Both optimise for volume of contact, not depth.

Pain B — the wrong team
65%

of high-potential startup failures come from people problems — co-founder conflict above all — not from product or market. The team decision is the highest-stakes one, made with the least information.

Noam Wasserman, The Founder's Dilemmas, Harvard Business School.

Phase 1 answers Pain A. Phase 2 answers Pain B.01
02 — Why now

The behaviour is already proven.
Just not here, and not for founders.

This is not a bet on whether people will pay to eat with strangers. That question has been answered at scale. The open question is whether a founder-specific version retains.

Proof of demand
€18M

Timeleft ARR, 20 months after launching weekly dinners between strangers. 150,000 diners a month, 200+ cities, ~100 staff.

Proof it starts small
€125

Timeleft's first week of revenue — a Typeform, a WhatsApp group and a Stripe link. No app. They ran to €1M/month on no-code.

The Indian market
2.23L

DPIIT-recognised startups in India; 55,200 added in FY26 alone, up 51.6% year on year. Delhi NCR is a top-three cluster.

The insight worth stealing: Timeleft treats the dinner itself as the technology — a table, five strangers, three hours, and the natural structure of a meal. No hosts, no ambassadors, no icebreakers. That is why it scales without a local team in every city, and it is the operating model Phase 1 copies.

Sources: Clef de voûte interview with Timeleft CEO (Oct 2025); PIB / DPIIT Startup India (Apr 2026)02
03 — The structure

One company. Two products.
The first one pays for the second.

Most founder-community businesses die trying to monetise introductions. This structure avoids that by selling dinners first and outcomes second — never the introduction itself.

Phase 1Months 0–12

The Table

  • Curated dinners and small events for founders and operators
  • Revenue from day one: seat fees and membership
  • Builds the only asset that matters — a trusted, verified roster
  • Low capital, no inventory, no venue ownership

Job: generate cash and earn trust at scale.

Phase 2Months 9–24

The Build

  • Structured co-founder formation sprints, invite-only
  • Recruited from Phase 1's roster — zero cold acquisition
  • Sells a formed team and a tested decision, never an introduction
  • Optional equity participation in teams that form

Job: convert trust into high-value outcomes.

Phase 2 without Phase 1 is a cold-start problem. Phase 1 without Phase 2 is a lifestyle business.03
Phase 104 — The Table

Six founders. One table.
Nobody pitching.

A weekly format with a hard constraint set. The constraints are the product — anyone can book a restaurant, almost nobody can guarantee who sits there.

The six seats
  • A stage you recognise — advice only lands between equals
  • A different industry — never a direct competitor
  • Someone further along the same road
  • Someone whose answers cut against yours
  • Nobody from your circle — same cap table, batch or contacts filtered out
  • You
The rules

No recording, no posting, no adding anyone to a CRM afterwards. No decks. No investors seated in disguise. Breaking it ends the membership.

The operating model

Guests pay the restaurant directly at menu price. We never touch F&B cost, never hold inventory, never guarantee a minimum spend. We sell the seat and the matching.

The constraint set is the defensible asset — not the venue, not the app.04
Phase 105 — The formats

Five formats, one roster.

Timeleft's members went from 1.4 to 2.2 experiences a month when formats were added beyond dinner. More formats is the single proven lever on retention and revenue per member.

Sequencing rule: launch with The Table only. Add one format per quarter, and only after the prior one clears 50 attendees a month. Timeleft opened 325 cities in a year and had to retreat to 220 — depth before breadth is their own stated correction.

Q1 — prove one format

The Table, weekly. Nothing else exists. The only metric that counts is whether people come back a second and third time.

Q2 — add depth, not breadth

The Post-Mortem. Same roster, different emotional register — and the format most likely to travel as content.

Q3 — add utility

The Workbench. Shifts the product from company to usefulness, which is what justifies a renewal in month nine.

Q4 — add reach

Long Table. The public face, the acquisition engine, and the room Phase 2 recruits from.

Every format runs on the same roster, the same matching engine and the same venue relationships.05

Phase 108 — The real risk

The only number that decides this.

Consumer membership communities churn at roughly 5.8% a month — about 51% a year — and low engagement is the top cancellation driver. Everything below is a function of where this lands.

Benchmark: membership-community churn studies, 2026. CAC of ₹300 assumed for Instagram-led acquisition in Indian metros.

Treat these ratios with suspicion

A 38× LTV:CAC is not a sign of genius — it is what any asset-light model shows before fixed cost and before churn is measured. The honest read: margin is comfortable, retention is unproven.

The test, before any build

Run one table. Pay for it yourself. Ask all six whether they would come again next week and pay for it. If fewer than four say yes, do not build the rest.

Consumer social products typically churn hardest at months 3–4 — that is the checkpoint that matters08
Phase 209 — The Build

Where you meet the person
you actually build with.

Phase 1 earns the right to do this. By month nine there is a verified roster of founders whose judgement, temperament and reliability have been observed in person — repeatedly, and by us.

What it is

A six-week structured sprint. Forty invited founders and senior operators, working in rotating pairs on real problems, with a formal decision point at the end: commit to a team, or walk away clean.

  • Invite-only, drawn from the Phase 1 roster
  • Weekly working sessions, not talks
  • Deliberate conflict testing built into the format
  • A written co-founder agreement template at the end
Why this is defensible

Anyone can run a mixer. What cannot be copied quickly is observed behaviour over months — who actually showed up, who listened, who was generous when nothing was at stake.

A matching platform has profiles. We have attendance records, host notes and peer feedback across dozens of dinners. That is a different asset class, and Phase 1 accumulates it as a by-product of making money.

Antler India runs 8-week residencies of 60–70 people; Entrepreneur First builds cofounding teams from individuals. The model is validated — the Indian mid-market is not served.09
10 — The mistake we are designing around

Lunchclub had everything.
It still died.

The autopsy
300,000 users
5,000,000 intros
$24M raised
Shut down

a16z-backed AI networking platform. Strong engagement, clean product, real usage. It could not convert any of it into revenue and closed quietly in 2022.

The pattern it belongs to

Bumble Bizz, discontinued in most regions. Professional networking bolted onto a consumer social product does not hold its own weight — which is why Phase 2 here is a separate product with its own price, not a feature of Phase 1.

Free, funded and widely used is not a business. It is a bill.

Why it failed

Networking value is diffuse and hard to attribute. A user who benefits cannot say which introduction did it, so willingness to pay stays near zero even among people who genuinely got value.

The lesson, verbatim

"User engagement metrics tell you whether you built something people use. They do not tell you whether you built something people will pay for."

What we do differently

We never sell an introduction. Phase 1 sells a seat at a specific dinner on a specific night — concrete, attributable, easy to price. Phase 2 sells a formed team and a tested decision, not access to a pool of profiles.

Source: UnicornBurn startup autopsy, Lunchclub (2018–2022)10
Phase 211 — Revenue lines

Three lines. Two of them cash.

The equity line is modelled for completeness but must never be counted on for near-term cash — it pays out in seven to ten years, if at all.

Line A — formation sprint
₹6.22L

contribution per cohort. 40 founders × ₹25,000, less ₹3.78L of venue, ops and curation over six weeks. 62% margin.

4 cohorts a year → ₹24.9L.

Line B — deal-flow partners
₹18L

per year from 6 VC or corporate partners at ₹3L each, for structured access to cohort demo sessions and the annual Long Table.

This is the line Lunchclub never built: funds have budget and attributable need.

Line C — equity (optional)
3–5%

in teams that formally form through a sprint. At 24 teams a year and an 8% hit rate against ₹20Cr outcomes, expected value is roughly ₹1.1Cr a year — realised a decade later.

Treat as upside, never as budget.

Pricing logic: ₹25,000 for six weeks is roughly a tenth of what the wrong co-founder costs in wasted months, and a rounding error against the 65% of failures attributable to people problems. Priced as insurance, not as an event ticket.

Why the cash lines come first

Lines A and B bill inside the quarter they are earned. A business that needs Line C to work is a fund, not a company.

What Line B actually sells

Not a logo on a page — structured access to teams a VC would otherwise meet six months later. A diligence shortcut, and worth paying for.

Cohort economics assume ₹25k venue and ₹18k ops per week, plus ₹1.2L curation and marketing per cohort11

13 — Go to market

Hand-built to 100. Paid after.

The first hundred members cannot be bought, because the product is who is in the room and paid acquisition cannot control that.

Stage 1 · Months 0–3 · to 100
  • Hand-pick and personally invite the first 60 from existing networks
  • Seed every table with one second-time founder, free
  • Zero ad spend — referral and invitation only
  • Run the Typeform + WhatsApp + UPI stack. No app, no build

Goal: 12 tables run, and a measured month-3 return rate.

Stage 2 · Months 4–9 · to 400
  • Instagram and LinkedIn creative built from real table footage
  • Founder-operator content: the post-mortem format is inherently shareable
  • Referral mechanic — a member who brings one pays nothing that month
  • Venue co-marketing: restaurants promote their own slow weeknights

Goal: CAC under ₹300 and churn under 10%.

Stage 3 · Months 9–24 · Phase 2
  • First sprint recruited entirely from the existing roster
  • Long Table becomes the public showcase and the VC touchpoint
  • Sign three deal-flow partners before running cohort two
  • Only then consider a second city

Goal: two sprints run, six teams formed.

Channel truth

Instagram carries the reach in Indian metros, but LinkedIn carries the credibility for a founder-gated product. Instagram for fill, LinkedIn for who fills it.

The creative that works

Not the food. The empty sixth chair, the rules, and one real post-mortem told plainly. Sell the constraint, never the restaurant.

Referral beats spend

A member who brings one pays nothing that month — ₹899 of margin for a pre-vetted member at roughly a third of paid CAC.

Channel priority follows Timeleft's own sequence: prove the format manually, then open with ads, then add formats for depth13
