01 — The cautionary tale

The best-funded company in this category
died with excellent metrics.

This slide comes first because it constrains every design decision that follows. Ignore it and you build the same company again.

The autopsy
300,000 users
5,000,000 intros
$24M raised
Shut down

a16z-backed AI networking platform. Strong engagement, clean product, real usage. It could not convert any of it into revenue and closed quietly in 2022.

Free, funded and widely used is not a business. It is a bill.

Why it failed — precisely

Not distribution. Not product quality. The matched party will not pay. The value of an introduction is real but diffuse — you cannot attribute your next job, customer or co-founder to the specific coffee that started it, so you will not pay a subscription for more coffees.

5 million introductions is more evidence than any startup could hope to gather, and the conclusion was unambiguous. The same pattern: Bumble Bizz, discontinued in most regions.

The three rules it forces on us

1 — Never charge for matching. Charge for a structured process with a defined end.

2 — Never rely on the matched party alone. Funds have budget and attributable need; founders do not.

3 — Never recruit strangers. Cold users have no trust to convert, which is why cold acquisition never paid for Lunchclub.

Every revenue and design decision in this deck traces back to one of these three rules01
02 — The problem

The highest-stakes decision a founder makes,
made on the least information.

Choosing a co-founder is a decade-long, equity-splitting, legally-entangled commitment — and it is routinely decided over three coffees and a shared enthusiasm.

The cost when it goes wrong
65%

of high-potential startup failures are attributable to people problems — co-founder conflict foremost — rather than product or market.

Noam Wasserman, The Founder's Dilemmas, Harvard Business School.

Why dating-app logic fails here

Matching on stated attributes — skills, sector, stage — predicts almost nothing about whether two people can survive a bad quarter together.

What predicts it is observed behaviour under disagreement, and no profile captures that.

Why existing options do not work
  • Accelerator matching: only if you are in the batch, and equity is already gone
  • Co-founder platforms: profile-based, free, and unvetted
  • Personal networks: small, and biased toward people like you

The reframe: the problem is not finding candidates — a founder can meet fifty on any platform. The problem is testing one properly before the commitment is irreversible. That is a process, it has a beginning and an end, and processes can be sold. Access cannot.

This is the pain Phase 1 deliberately does not address — different problem, different product02
03 — The product

Six weeks. Build something real, together.

40 founders per cohort, drawn from the Phase 1 roster. Paired and re-paired against a structured sequence designed to surface conflict early, while it is still cheap.

WeekFocusWhat actually happensWhat it reveals
1CalibrationStructured sessions on money, ambition, exit horizon, and what each person will not compromise onMismatches that would surface in year two, surfaced in week one
2RotationEach founder works a short problem with three different potential partnersWho you work well with, as distinct from who you enjoy talking to
3–4The buildSelf-selected pairs ship something small and real in two weeksPace, follow-through, and how each handles being blocked
5The stress testA deliberately adversarial session: equity split, role definition, a simulated bad quarterConflict behaviour — the single most predictive signal, and the one nobody tests for
6DecisionCommit, part ways, or re-pair. Demo session in front of partner fundsA decision made on evidence rather than enthusiasm
Parting ways is a success

A pair that separates in week five has avoided a two-year mistake for ₹25,000. We say this explicitly in the marketing, because it is the honest frame and it lowers the barrier to entry.

Why six weeks

Long enough for behaviour under pressure to appear — short enough that it costs a founder a manageable slice of a quarter. Accelerator-length programmes are not viable for people already running something.

Week 5 is the whole product

Everything before it is setup. The adversarial session is where equity, roles and a simulated bad quarter get argued in a room with a facilitator — the conversation founders otherwise have for the first time when it is already too late.

Cohort size 40 is set by matching mathematics: enough combinations to re-pair twice without exhausting the pool03
04 — The unfair advantage

We have already watched them argue.

Every competitor in co-founder matching starts with a profile a stranger wrote about themselves. We start with months of observed behaviour, and that difference is the whole business.

01
Pre-vetted, not self-declared

Every candidate is ID-verified with proof-of-building already checked, because Phase 1 required it to sit at a table. Zero fake profiles, zero tyre-kickers, zero verification cost at this stage.

02
Behavioural signal

Phase 1 deliberately seats one person at each table who disagrees with you. Over eight dinners we have watched how each member handles being contradicted, being wrong, and being outranked — the exact signal co-founder matching needs and cannot otherwise obtain.

03
Trust that converts

A ₹25,000 six-week commitment is an easy sell to someone who has had eight good dinners with us, and a near-impossible one to cold traffic. This is the conversion Lunchclub never had.

Why this cannot be copied by a well-funded competitor: they would have to run the dinners first. The behavioural data is a by-product of a business that takes two years to build and earns its own margin along the way. A competitor who skips Phase 1 arrives with profiles and cold traffic — which is exactly the position Lunchclub occupied with $24M.

The assumption that decides this

Can we fill 40 seats at ₹25,000? At a 650-member Phase 1 roster, that is a 6% conversion into a cohort. Plausible — but unmeasured, and everything here rests on it.

Test it cheaply: run cohort one at 20 seats and ₹15,000. Lower revenue, same learning, a third of the downside. If 20 seats will not fill from a warm roster, 40 never will.

Read the margin carefully. 62% is healthy but it is not a software margin — this product consumes six weeks of venue, facilitation and curation per cohort, and that cost scales linearly with cohorts run. Phase 2 is a high-value services business, not a platform. Modelling it as a platform is how the plan would go wrong.

07 — The partner line

The customer who actually has budget.

Line B is the structural answer to Lunchclub. Rather than asking the matched party to pay for a diffuse benefit, sell an attributable one to a party that already budgets for it.

What a fund pays ₹3L for
  • Demo session access at the end of each cohort — four a year
  • Teams observed working together for six weeks, not pitching for six minutes
  • First conversation before the team is formally raising
  • Two seats at the annual Long Table

₹3L is a rounding error against a single analyst's salary, and funds already spend far more than this on sourcing.

Why this sale is easier

A fund's need is attributable: they can point at a deal and trace it to a source. That is the precise property founder-side networking lacks, and the reason founders will not pay a subscription for introductions.

It is also budgeted: sourcing is an existing line item, so we are competing for allocation, not creating a new category of spend.

The honest risk

Funds pay for proven deal flow. Cohort one has no track record, so the first two or three partners will be relationship sales at a discount, or free.

Sequencing: sign three partners before running cohort two, priced at whatever the first year is genuinely worth. Full ₹3L pricing is a year-two number, and modelling it in year one would be dishonest.

How to open the conversation: not with a sponsorship deck. Invite three partners to observe one cohort's demo session for free, then price year two against what they saw. A fund that has watched six teams work for six weeks needs no explanation of what the access is worth.

Partner count assumption: 6 by end of year two. Year one realistically lands at 3, discounted.07
08 — Who this is for

Recruited from inside, not advertised outside.

The recruitment channel is the product's main quality control. Opening this to cold applicants would remove the only advantage it has.

Primary — 80% of each cohort

Phase 1 members who have signalled they are looking for a co-founder. Already ID-verified, already observed across multiple tables, already paying us monthly.

Acquisition cost: effectively zero. They are on a WhatsApp list we already own.

Secondary — 20%, by referral

Technical founders brought in to balance the skill mix, referred by existing members and vetted to the same standard.

Necessary because Phase 1 will skew commercial, and a cohort of forty business founders forms no companies.

The three admission tests
  • Free to commit. Not currently bound to an existing co-founder or a full-time job they will not leave.
  • Specific about the gap. "I need a technical co-founder for this" beats "I want to meet people."
  • Able to ship in two weeks. Weeks 3–4 require actually building something.

The 80/20 split is deliberate. A cohort recruited entirely from Phase 1 would inherit its commercial skew and form no companies. A cohort recruited mostly from outside would lose the behavioural signal that makes this product work at all. Twenty percent is the largest external share that preserves the advantage.

No paid acquisition for Phase 2 in year one — a cold applicant has none of the signal this product depends on08

10 — What kills this

Four risks, and the order matters.

The first one is inherited from Phase 1 and is the reason the gate on the next slide exists.

1 — Phase 1 does not retain
Fatal

No roster means recruiting strangers, which means becoming Lunchclub. This risk is entirely outside Phase 2's control.

Mitigation: the launch gate. Do not start until Phase 1 shows a stable month-6 cohort.

2 — Cohorts do not fill

40 seats at ₹25,000 requires 6% conversion from a 650-member roster. Plausible, unmeasured.

Mitigation: run cohort one at 20 seats and ₹15,000. Same learning, a third of the downside.

3 — No teams actually form

If six weeks produces no companies, Line B collapses — funds are buying deal flow that does not exist.

Mitigation: market parting ways as a success. The insurance framing survives a low formation rate; a deal-flow framing does not.

4 — A team forms and fails badly

Reputational, and the first one will happen. We will be blamed for a pairing we facilitated.

Mitigation: never claim to guarantee compatibility. The product is a structured test, and the founders make the decision. Say this in writing, at signup.

The stop rule: if cohort one fills fewer than 12 of 20 seats from a warm roster, do not run cohort two. That is a demand signal, not a marketing problem, and spending against it would be the expensive mistake.

Risks 2 and 3 are both testable inside a single discounted cohort10
11 — Sequencing

Do not build this yet.

This is the most important slide in the deck, and it argues against its own product. Phase 2 is the more interesting business and the more defensible one — which is exactly why it must not be started early.

The gate

Phase 2 does not launch until Phase 1 has:

  • A stable month-6 cohort — members who have stayed half a year
  • At least 300 active members on the roster
  • Churn measured and under 12%
  • A written matching rubric, not personal taste

All four. Not three.

Why the temptation is dangerous

Phase 2 has better margins per customer, a clearer story for investors, and more obvious defensibility. Every instinct will say start it sooner.

But its entire advantage — the vetted roster, the behavioural data, the trust that converts a ₹25,000 sale — is produced by Phase 1 and cannot be bought. Starting early means buying cold users instead, and that is Lunchclub's exact position with $24M behind it.

The sequence is not caution. It is the only version of this that works.

Realistic timing: if Phase 1 launches now and retains, the gate opens around month 9–12. Cohort one runs in month 12–14 at reduced size and price. Full-scale cohorts from month 18.
